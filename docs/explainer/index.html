A pilot flies the city. A filter keeps a way out.

Every 20 ms the city flight demo's safety filter asks one question: after the pilot's next input, could the aircraft still fly an escape that clears every building and hillside? If yes, the pilot keeps control. If no, the filter flies the escape it already checked. Tested, not proved: no crashes in 198 verified starts, 2,640 adversary flights or 5 kamikaze runs.1

The problem

The demo puts a Cessna 172 in a procedural city: a 6 km square map with a downtown of towers up to about 450 m, streets 28 m wide between some of them, and a ridge beyond.1 The aircraft is JSBSim's c172p, run through JsbExact, a Lean port that reproduces JSBSim 1.3.3 bit for bit on every compared step of its validation data.2

Anyone can fly it, with a keyboard, a script, or an autopilot whose only goal is to hit something. The filter sits between the stick and the aircraft, and it has two jobs that pull against each other:

  • Never let the aircraft touch anything, whatever the pilot does next.
  • Stay out of the way as long as it can. A filter that takes over whenever a wall is close would never let anyone fly a street canyon, where the walls are always close.

The design that reconciles the two is a gatekeeper. It does not ask how close the walls are. It asks whether a way out still exists after the pilot's next input, and steps in only at the last tick where the answer is still yes.

What the filter must guarantee

The target is a single statement, Safe F: no state the closed loop can reach from an allowed start is a crash.3 Three choices in that statement matter.

  • The pilot is arbitrary: any control-surface and throttle input in its physical range, chosen afresh every JSBSim frame (1/128 s). The keyboard pilot, the fly-by-wire loop and the kamikaze autopilot are special cases.
  • A crash is actual contact, between the airframe and a building or the terrain. There is no clearance margin in the definition.
  • Starts are a small, conservative set, such as steady level flight well clear of everything. Because the pilot is arbitrary, the close calls below are still reachable from such starts.

Terminal sets, "safe forever" sets and flight-envelope limits are deliberately left out of the statement. A proof may use them; the target does not depend on them.3

How the gate decides, every 20 ms

The filter predicts with the aircraft itself. Instead of a simplified model, each check copies the exact JsbExact state and flies the copy forward, one JSBSim frame at a time.1 One tick of the filter:

  1. Copy the aircraft's full state.
  2. Fly the pilot's input on the copy for one 20 ms tick.
  3. Then fly an escape on the copy, frame by frame, for up to 16 s. The escape tried first is the last one that passed; if it fails, the others are tried in turn while the tick's frame budget lasts.
  4. Check clearance every 8 frames (1/16 s, about 3 m at 50 m/s): the airframe, a 4.5 m body sphere plus a 0.8 m capsule along the 11 m wing, must stay more than 4 m from every building box and the terrain.
  5. Look for an ending that stays safe, from 6 s into the escape: a climbing ray that clears everything until it is above the map's highest point or off the map, or a steady climbing helix whose ground ring is clear at the current height.
  6. Decide. If some escape passes, the pilot's input goes to the real aircraft. If none does, the aircraft flies the escape that passed on the previous tick, which was checked from exactly the state the aircraft is now in.

Because the committed escape is replayed on the same plant from the same state, it flies exactly the frames that were checked. Each tick spends at most 2,200 JSBSim frames; measured single-threaded on a loaded machine, a tick took 6.4 ms of CPU time at the median and 19.9 ms at the 99th percentile, against a 20 ms tick.1

The scenario starts the aircraft at 45 m/s, 88 m above a 28 m wide street that runs north between a 261 m and a 292 m tower, then between a 235 m and a 352 m tower. The scripted showcase pilot holds the centreline through both gaps, then banks hard right into the 352 m tower and holds the bank for 5 s.1

The closest approach, 5.61 m, stays above the 4 m every checked rollout must keep at its samples. That 4 m is a design margin, not a bound: the Rust gate checks clearance only every 1/16 s along a rollout, and nothing bounds it in between.

The evidence

The gate is tested, and the tests are adversarial. The main harness flies 200 hostile episodes from random starts (a quarter with the kamikaze autopilot), plus the kamikaze autopilot in each named scenario, all on the JSBSim aircraft. A start counts as verified when the filter accepts the pilot's first tick.1

Other attacks

  • Adversary search: 0 crashes in 2,640 gap-guided flights from 200 verified starts.1
  • Tower gap: 0 crashes in 100 runs (50 hostile, 50 kamikaze), all from verified starts. The showcase pilot's 60 s run commits 245 of 3,000 ticks in a replay of the current code (the README's table, from an earlier version, says 208).1
  • Through the packaged library (libgate, the form other simulators link): 0 crashes from 45 verified starts with pilots driving raw control surfaces, and 20 with fly-by-wire pilots.4
  • Not budget starvation: with an unlimited per-tick budget the filter still flies 29.5% of ticks, so the interventions are what the escapes require.1

The point-mass row is the comparison to read first. That filter predicts with a simplified model of the aircraft; flown on JSBSim's c172p, it crashed in 27 of 200 episodes. The gate predicts with the plant itself.

What is exact, what is numerical, what is proved

Each part of the gate's argument sits on one rung. Nothing about the gate itself is on the top rung yet.4

Provedkernel-checked Lean theorem

  • Nothing about the gate.
  • Elsewhere on main: Envelope.never_hit, a no-hit theorem for a different, longitudinal envelope filter over the terrain grid. It does not cover buildings or the 6-DOF aircraft.

Exactmatches the flown aircraft frame for frame

  • Rollouts. Each check forks the JsbExact state and steps it frame by frame, so a committed escape flies exactly the frames that were checked.
  • JsbExact equals JSBSim 1.3.3's c172p (its macOS arm64 build) bit for bit on every compared step of the validation datasets. That equivalence is measured, not proved.

Numericalchecked by sampling

  • Clearance along a rollout: sampled every 8 JSBSim frames, must exceed 4 m there. Nothing bounds it between samples; the margin is sized by testing.
  • "Safe forever" endings: the climbing ray and the steady helix are checked on the settled rollout, not proved to hold as the aircraft flies on.
  • Map checks read a 2.5 m height raster; leaving the 6 km map counts as clear.

Arguedin code comments

  • The induction: an escape that passed its check always exists, because the filter only accepts an input after which one passes, and otherwise flies the one it already has.
  • The filter and escapes are Rust, outside Lean; no theorem mentions JsbExact yet.

What's next

The proof route laid out in docs/gate-proof-status.md:4

  1. Enclose JsbExact over sets of states. Its data-dependent branches already go through a Scalar class, so an interval instance can be sound, and one escape frame can be enclosed over a box of states.
  2. Prove a terminal set invariant around the climbing ray or the helix, replacing the numerical "safe forever" endings.
  3. Make the rollout checks sound: bound clearance between samples, for example a speed bound times the sample interval inside the margin, and show the raster tests are conservative.
  4. Formalise the induction and the hand-back rule, as Envelope.never_hit does for the envelope filter.
  5. Close the code gap: port the escape law and checks to Lean, or check the Rust against a Lean reference by differential testing.

On the demo side, tighter close calls than the ones above are being searched for and can replace these exhibits. The plate above is generated from a replay (docs/explainer/plate_data.py), so a new exhibit is one replay away.
